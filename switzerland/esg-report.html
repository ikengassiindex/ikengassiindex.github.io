Why an ESG report for Switzerland's grid?

Switzerland's power grid sits at the meeting point of four structural pressures: an alpine topography that concentrates the highest seismic hazard in DACH (Wallis PGA 0.16 g, 475-year return) and significant flash-flood exposure (Rhône, Aare, Rhine, Ticino catchments), a hydropower-dominant generation mix (~58% of national output) that ties grid reliability to glacial baseflow and pumped-storage cycling, a non-EU regulatory posture that maintains continental synchronous operation through bilateral exchange agreements rather than full single-market integration, and the most-interconnected per-population border footprint in the OECD (41 cross-border lines with 5 neighbours). This report quantifies how those pressures translate into measurable Environmental, Social, and Governance exposure at substation resolution — across all 26 Kantone — using only public, citable data sources.

The report follows the eight-topic structure required under CSRD/ESRS limited assurance equivalence: Climate, Air Quality, Water, Biodiversity, Pollution, Land Use, Workforce, and Community. Each topic provides a narrative anchored in Swiss federal institutional sources (ElCom, BFS/OFS, MeteoSwiss, SED, swisstopo, BAFU, Pronovo, NCSC, Swissgrid), a data table populated from the SSI scoring engine, a cross-country comparison versus DACH and OECD peers, and a methodology pointer. The Markov degradation profile and physical-risk classes (Climate Section) follow ISO 9223 and CIGRE TB 761; corrosion classes are calibrated for Swiss conditions — landlocked geography eliminates C5 marine exposure, but alpine valley + urban industrial clusters span C2 through C4.

Report 1 · ESRS E1 equivalence · TCFD Physical Risk · ISO 14091

1. Climate Physical Risk Assessment

Switzerland's grid faces a continental-alpine climate-risk profile that combines the highest seismic exposure in DACH with a uniquely concentrated alpine-valley flood pattern. The 2024 summer flood window (June–August) delivered three distinct events across the Lake Lucerne, Linth and Saane catchments; Swissgrid recorded approximately 11 substation-hours of weather-driven outage with no asset losses, anchoring the country's reference event for CMIP6-scaled projections. This report uses ERA5 reanalysis (31 km, hourly, 1940–present) combined with CMIP6 RCP4.5 projections to 2050, calibrated to the MeteoSwiss national grid, and overlays the SED ESHM20 + national PGA atlas on substation coordinates. Geological context comes from swisstopo's 1:25,000 maps and BAFU geomorphology layers.

Material flood exposure is concentrated in four corridors: the Rhône (Wallis to Lake Geneva), the Aare (Bern and Aargau), the Rhine (Basel and downstream to the German border), and the Ticino (south-alpine valleys). MeteoSwiss GHK (Gefahrenhinweiskarten) maps are the authoritative national hazard layer for R6c_flood calibration. Seismic alpha for R6_seismic falls in the [0.04, 0.22] range — the highest in DACH — with Wallis at PGA 0.16 g (475-year return), Basel + Graubünden + Ticino at 0.12 g, and northern Cantons (Aargau, Schaffhausen, Thurgau, Zürich) at 0.04–0.08 g. The 1946 Sierre quake (M_L 5.8) is the largest instrumental Swiss reference. The Markov 5-state degradation model (CIGRE TB 761) is calibrated at high tier for Switzerland: continental synchronous grid (via bilateral exchange agreements) since 1909, mature observational record from MeteoSwiss and SED, no recent regime change. Aletsch + Rhône glacier retreat — CMIP6 RCP4.5 projects 50% mass loss by 2050 — reshapes Wallis hydropower seasonality and shifts R6_climate weight from winter (snow loading) toward summer (drought-stress on hydropower availability).

Report 2 · ESRS E2 equivalence · Swiss Ordinance on Air Pollution Control (OAPC, LRV)

2. Air Quality

Atmospheric quality drives both human-health policy obligations and asset-level corrosion. BAFU operates the NABEL federal monitoring network (16 reference stations) plus a dense cantonal network (~120 additional sites) coordinated by the federal-cantonal Cercl'Air association. Three distinctive Swiss patterns shape the substation corrosion calibration: (i) urban PM10 + NO₂ in Zürich, Bern, Geneva and Lausanne — generally compliant with the federal limit values but episodic in winter inversions, (ii) alpine-valley NO₂ inversion in the Rhône, Reuss and Inn valleys — particularly in the Wallis section and in Graubünden — where katabatic flows concentrate traffic and heating emissions across multi-day episodes, and (iii) industrial corrosion uplift in the Basel chemical-pharma cluster (Roche, Novartis, Syngenta) and the Aarau industrial corridor. These patterns map directly to substation corrosion class: most Swissgrid and DSO assets carry ISO 9223 C2; alpine valleys (Wallis, Graubünden, Ticino, Uri, Glarus) are uprated to C3; Zürich + Basel urban cores and the industrial Aarau cluster sit at C4. Switzerland's landlocked geography means no fleet assets are in C5 marine territory.

Report 3 · ESRS E3 equivalence · Swiss Federal Waters Protection Act (GSchG) · SFDR PAI #8 Water

3. Water

Water and the Swiss grid are inseparable: hydropower accounts for approximately 58% of Swiss electricity generation, with Axpo, Alpiq and BKW anchoring the alpine cascade. Pumped storage adds 5.3 GW across four key plants — Linth-Limmern, Nant de Drance, Grimsel, and Vieux-Émosson — making Switzerland the largest pumped-storage operator per capita in the OECD. Substations in this report inherit water-related risk from three sources — (i) cooling and dielectric water for switchyard auxiliaries (negligible in Switzerland, since hydropower dominates and thermal cooling is minimal beyond the four remaining nuclear plants until phase-out 2034), (ii) hydropower-driven generation variability that propagates back into HV transmission stress, particularly during summer drought episodes and during pumped-storage daily cycling, and (iii) flood proximity for substations in the Rhône, Aare, Rhine, Reuss, Limmat and Ticino floodplains. The MeteoSwiss GHK atlas is the authoritative national hazard layer used in the SSI flood-exposure module; Swissgrid and the regional DSOs (BKW, Romande Énergie, IWB, EWZ, Repower, CKW, plus the long tail of ~550 cantonal-municipal utilities) coordinate flood-defence retrofit through ElCom's network-development assessment cycle.

Report 4 · ESRS E4 equivalence · Bern Convention Emerald Network · Federal Inventories

4. Biodiversity

Switzerland is not subject to EU Natura 2000 but operates an equivalent national framework: the Emerald Network under the Bern Convention plus the federal inventories for landscapes (BLN), dryland sites (TWW), alluvial zones, fens, raised bogs, and amphibian spawning sites — coordinated by BAFU and the Cantons. Combined coverage of biodiversity-protected land sits at approximately 11% of national territory (lower than EU peers because federal protection covers fewer sites at a higher quality bar). SSI biodiversity exposure is calculated as the share of substation buffer area (500 m) intersecting Emerald + BLN polygons and the count of red-list endemic species within the catchment. The acute biodiversity stress in Switzerland is alpine: endemics in the Swiss National Park (Engadin), the Aletsch UNESCO area, and the Greina plateau are under climate pressure as treeline rises (~250 m per decade); substation footprints in those corridors carry a higher cantonal-permitting bar, with BAFU and the cantonal Naturschutzfachstellen coordinating EIA-equivalent assessments. The Rhône valley substations near the Pfynwald-Finges nature park and the Bolle di Magadino in Ticino attach additional constraints to network-development planning.

Report 5 · ESRS E2 Pollution equivalence · Swiss Federal Industrial Emissions Reporting

5. Pollution

Switzerland's industrial-emission profile is dominated by two clusters: the Basel chemical-pharma triangle (Roche, Novartis, Syngenta — combined ~3.2 Mt/y CO₂ across direct + electricity-indirect Scope 2) and the Holcim Untervaz cement plant in Graubünden (~2.5 Mt/y, the single largest national CO₂ point source). Both clusters are committed to deep decarbonisation, with Holcim piloting carbon capture at Untervaz (CCS commissioning target 2028) and the Basel pharma triangle accelerating green-electricity sourcing under the federal "Net Zero by 2050" trajectory. From a grid perspective, this matters in two ways: (i) the substations serving these clusters carry heightened transition-stress signatures (T1 component) as electrification scales, and (ii) corrosion class is uprated to C4 in the immediate industrial footprint due to airborne SO₂ and particulate deposition. Beyond the major clusters, transformer mineral-oil leaks and SF₆ fugitive emissions are tracked at fleet level — Swissgrid reports both to ElCom and the federal pollutant register (SwissPRTR) annually.

Report 6 · ESRS E4 Land Use equivalence · Swiss Spatial Planning Act (RPG) · Constitutional Soil Protection

6. Land Use

Swiss land-use policy is among the tightest in Europe — anchored in the constitutional principle of no net soil loss by 2050 (adopted by federal referendum in 2013) and operationalised through the revised Spatial Planning Act (RPG, in force since 2014). Inner densification has been the federal mandate for a decade, and greenfield land-take has fallen from ~1.0 m²/second in the 2010s to ~0.6 m²/second in 2024. This directly constrains greenfield substation expansion and pushes Swissgrid and the DSOs toward brownfield reuse, voltage upgrades, and underground cabling — particularly in the alpine valleys and the central Mittelland corridor. The critical projects for transmission capacity are the Gotthard north-south HVDC strengthening (Swissgrid Strategic Grid 2025 plan) and the Chamoson–Chippis 380 kV upgrade in Wallis (commissioned 2024). The Pfynwald-Finges and the Greina plateau Emerald sites are the two land-use-pressure hotspots that constrain alpine-corridor planning.

Report 7 · ESRS S1 Workforce equivalence · NCSC ICT-Minimum Standard 2018 + CYBER 2025

7. Workforce

Swissgrid employs approximately 720 staff across system operation, asset management, planning and corporate functions; the combined Swiss DSO workforce (BKW, Romande Énergie, IWB, EWZ, Repower, CKW, EKZ, SIG, AEW, Groupe E and the ~550 cantonal-municipal tail) stands at approximately 12,000 employees. The dominant workforce risk is demographic: skilled-trades roles — overhead-line technicians, substation electricians, protection engineers — face an aging pyramid with a Q4 2024 median age above 47 and Electrosuisse (the Swiss federation of electrical engineers) flagging a multi-year shortfall in apprenticeship intake, particularly in the Romand cantons and in Ticino. The R7 ceiling — which combines NCSC ICT-Minimum Standard 2018 compliance and the CYBER 2025 programme posture (deeper SCADA segmentation and post-quantum cryptography roadmaps) — caps at 1.05 for Switzerland. There is no DESI score for Switzerland (non-EU); the R7 ceiling is set by NCSC posture and ENISA observer-track collaboration, mid-band by DACH standards.

Report 8 · ESRS S2 Affected Communities equivalence · UN PRI · Swiss Electricity Supply Act (StromVG)

8. Community

ElCom mandates quarterly stakeholder reporting from Swissgrid and the DSOs — covering planned outages, restoration performance, affordability, and renewable-connection queues. The Pronovo renewable register is the single national counter for guarantees-of-origin (GoO) and feed-in support tracking — administering both the legacy KEV/Einmalvergütung schemes and the new market-premium regime. Switzerland's community-energy adoption follows a different legal frame from the EU citizen-energy-community model: the federally regulated "Eigenverbrauchsgemeinschaft" (ZEV) and the newer "Lokale Elektrizitätsgemeinschaft" (LEG, introduced 2025 under the revised StromVG) enable in-building and neighbourhood self-consumption pooling. As of 2024, more than 7,500 ZEV/LEG arrangements serve approximately 180,000 participants across the 26 Kantone, with the strongest densities in Bern, Vaud and Zürich. The SSI integrates these counts into the R3 social modifier and the V_socio energy-poverty index, surfacing where grid-investment priorities and community-affordability pressures align. Swiss energy-poverty rate (~3.0% by federal estimate) remains low by European standard.
