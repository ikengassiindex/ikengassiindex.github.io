4. Data Source Assessment by Tier

Tier 1: Primary Sources (10 sources, ~65% of variables)

Open access, high authority, Denmark-specific. DENMARK-NATIVE sources (Energinet, Forsyningstilsynet, Danmarks Statistik, DMI, GEUS, Miljøstyrelsen, Kystdirektoratet, CFCS, OSM, Copernicus).

Tier 2: Secondary Sources (10 sources, ~25% of variables)

Modeled, interpolated, or regional surveys. DSOs (RADIUS, N1, Cerius, NOE, KONSTANT, Energi Fyn, others), ENTSO-E, Geodatastyrelsen, Dansk Energi, Nationalbanken, Energistyrelsen, Dansk Fjernvarme, Nord Pool, Eurostat, SolarGIS.

Tier 3: Proxy / Fallback (2–3 sources, ~10% of variables)

Substation vintage (OSM start_date + cohort models), critical infrastructure (spatial analysis + population density), equipment condition (age curves + ambient temp + coastal storm hazard exposure).

5. Key Advantages & Challenges

Advantages

  • Energinet transparency: Public data portal with real-time dispatch, 2 bidding zones (DK1 West, DK2 East), wind forecasts, and cross-border flows. Strong integration with Nordic market and EU transparency platforms.
  • Register-based Census (DST): Denmark's continuous CPR register system provides granular demographic updates at kommune level (98 units) without census gaps. Real-time employment, income, and migration tracking via tax registers. Gold standard in Northern Europe.
  • DMI meteorology: ~100 weather stations with excellent historical records and superior wind forecasting (critical for 80%+ wind+solar penetration). North Sea wave data integration. Open API with no licensing barriers.
  • Wind energy leadership: Denmark world leader in wind penetration (~80% on recent windy days). Energistyrelsen wind registry provides project visibility. Offshore wind expansion major growth vector. Key for T1 (generation variability) and renewable integration modeling.
  • District heating coupling: Highest district heating penetration in Europe (~60% residential). Dansk Fjernvarme and CHP data enable unique energy system integration analysis; thermal demand side representation.
  • Coastal hazard expertise: Kystdirektoratet and Miljøstyrelsen provide comprehensive coastal flooding, storm surge, and sea level rise mapping. Critical for low-lying island and peninsula infrastructure.
  • Small geography, high density: Compact country (~43,000 km²) enables dense data coverage. ~2,000–3,500 HV substations manageable for granular asset-level analysis. Urban infrastructure density supports detailed SCADA integration.
  • Solar growth potential: Increasing solar capacity (6+ GW target by 2030). Global Solar Atlas + SolarGIS provide high-resolution irradiance data. Complementary to wind for renewable smoothing.

Challenges

  • DSO fragmentation: ~40 active DSOs with variable reporting standards and asset transparency. No centralized asset database equivalent to larger countries. Consolidation trend ongoing but slow; regional cooperation improving.
  • Wind variability & ramp rates: Denmark's 80%+ wind penetration creates extreme generation variability. Hourly ramps can exceed 50% of instantaneous load. High wind forecasting precision required; current models struggle with coastal/island effects.
  • Interconnection bottlenecks: DK1–DK2 internal transmission constraint during peak wind and demand periods. Limited east-west transmission capacity. Cross-border congestion with Germany (2 AC, 1 DC) and Sweden common during export periods.
  • Soft soil foundation risks: Significant portions of Denmark underlain by soft clay and glacial deposits. Substation foundation settlement and ground water rise risks in low-lying areas and islands. Limited geotechnical baseline data for all infrastructure.
  • Coastal flood exposure: ~60% of critical infrastructure and population within 10 km of coast. Sea level rise (Denmark among most vulnerable in Europe) and storm surge risks increasing. Kystdirektoratet data improving but gaps in subsidence/relative sea level projections.
  • Substation vintage data gaps: OSM coverage ~80–85% HV but completeness variable in rural/island areas. Individual DSO asset databases not universally public; age cohort models required. Island infrastructure (Bornholm, Faroe Islands) less documented.
  • District heating data fragmentation: ~100+ independent district heating companies with inconsistent data sharing. CHP coupling data scattered; no centralized efficiency metrics or thermal demand aggregation.
  • Island electrification challenges: Bornholm and remote islands subject to extreme weather exposure, supply chain constraints, and variable generation (local wind). Limited redundancy; single-point-of-failure transmission connections to mainland.

6. Estimated Timeline & Ingestion Phases

All 95 variables status: PLANNED (pre-ingestion phase). Once data ingestion commences, expect phased rollout over 4–6 months:
  • Phase 1 (Week 1–4): Core sources (D01–D10): Energinet, Forsyningstilsynet, DST, DMI, GEUS, Miljøstyrelsen, Kystdirektoratet, CFCS, OSM. ~60 variables mapped to 5 Regioner + 2 bidding zones.
  • Phase 2 (Week 5–8): Secondary tier (D11–D20): DSOs, ENTSO-E, Geodatastyrelsen, Dansk Energi, Nationalbanken, Energistyrelsen, Dansk Fjernvarme, Nord Pool, Eurostat, SolarGIS. ~25 variables for 98 Kommuner.
  • Phase 3 (Week 9–16): Markov model (F/I), Monte Carlo engine, validation across ~2,000–3,500 HV substations. Nordic/EU market integration (ENTSO-E, Nord Pool, cross-border flow analysis). DK1–DK2 balancing and internal constraints.
  • Phase 4 (Week 17–24): Dashboard deployment, ESG enrichment, intelligence page monthly updates. Cross-border coordination testing (Sweden, Germany, Nordic grid). Hazard zone scenario planning (coastal flood, wind ramp, soft soil, island resilience). Wind variability and CHP coupling integration.
